# Engine strength audit — 15 October 2026

High5 has a functioning board, threat detector and PVS search, but its position model and tactical horizon are much less informative than PentaZen's and Rapfi's. The likely strength bottleneck is the combination: weak evaluation ranks developing positions poorly, coarse move scores feed aggressive pruning, and shallow forcing search discovers losses after preventive defence was needed. These are source-supported explanations, not measured Elo contributions for individual features, with one equal-time measurement added by the second pass: on the loss-derived position suite the current pruning saves fewer positions than switching it off (finding 4).

The audit inspected all production Rust modules, tests, the match driver, the position suite, and the local PentaZen and Rapfi sources. It independently checked the latest Tito match and replayed four positions against the supplied executables. No production engine code or settings were changed.

A second pass on the same day extended the audit with an equal-time ablation of the pruning switches on the position suite, a node-distribution breakdown of every decision in the same Tito match, a line-by-line check of the borrowed move-count formulas, and an inventory of the search heuristics Rapfi reports as large Elo gains. It reproduced the first pass's match statistics, probe values and 51 passing tests, and again changed no production engine code or settings. The second pass's measurements appear in the evidence breakdown below, in finding 4 and its table, in findings 3, 5 and 6, in the new finding 8, and in the implementation order.

## Evidence from the current match

`logs/pbrain-Tito2014_20261014_235818/results.csv` confirms **3 wins, 17 losses, no draws or errors**: 3–7 as first player, 0–10 as second. All 20 PSQ records contain legal placements and finish at the first five, with the winner matching the result file. All ten pairs use identical three-stone openings. The logs specify `START 15`, freestyle rule 0, 1000 ms per turn and 300000 ms per match. This run provides evidence of playing weakness rather than a size, colour mapping, opening pairing or result-classification failure.

Among 232 non-mate decisions with nonzero recorded thinking time, high5 averages depth **7.65**, **828 ms**, and approximately **0.79 million counted nodes/s**. Tito reports an average depth of **11.46** and **2.82 million nodes/s** over 180 reported searches. Depth and node accounting differ between engines, so these are diagnostic observations rather than directly comparable measures of strength. High5 recognizes a mate loss in every lost game; the median board size at its first such report is only **19 stones**.

**65.96% of high5's counted nodes are leaf-search calls.** This is a node fraction, not a measurement of wall time. 92.79% of main-search beta cutoffs occur on the first move; that statistic by itself does not establish good strategic ordering when evaluation and pruning share the same coarse model.

Counted nodes are dominated by the horizon. Leaf-search calls are **66.0%** of them and leaf plus horizon-entry nodes **87.7%**, leaving only about **12%** interior nodes, which is the only place where the move ordering, the move-count limit, the late move reductions and the TT lookups act. The other 88% of the tree is decided by the leaf four-search and the static evaluation rather than by the pruning, which is why findings 1 and 3 outweigh any main-search knob (findings 4 and 8). The same log gives the depth reached on the 232 non-mate decisions: 5 in 1, 6 in 12, 7 in 87, 8 in 107, 9 in 21 and above 9 in 4. Of those 232, 128 used at least 900 ms of the 950 ms budget while 31 returned in under 600 ms.

## Comparison of implemented mechanisms

| Mechanism | High5 | PentaZen source | Rapfi source / supplied configuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static evaluation | Four untuned counts of possible threat-making cells | Existing line material from blocked/open ones through fours; incremental scores | Four-direction pattern combinations, learned classical tables, side-to-move threat table; supplied binary loads NNUE |
| Fork information | Each threat type is one Boolean per cell, merged across directions | Per-direction material changes summed in move scores | Explicit double-three, four-three, double-four and weaker combination classes |
| Leaf tactics | At most one voluntary four by default; any board-wide four tried in bit order | Selective continuous-four win search with depth up to `min(2*ply,120)` and localized, ranked continuation candidates | Recursive VCF, localized/ranked moves, combination filtering at deeper continuation levels, TT and delta pruning |
| Main-search forcing depth | Block of a four costs a ply; open-three replies are free, capped at root ply 64 | Four blocks receive a one-ply extension | Four blocks receive a 1.3-ply extension; additional selective extensions |
| Move pruning | Counts and reductions driven by coarse masks/history; deeper nodes may discard threatening moves too | Similar count formulas alongside richer directional scoring and cautious win verification | Pattern, policy, history, complexity and improvement inform search selectivity |
| Search heuristics | PVS, killers and a per-cell history only; no null move, futility, razoring, aspiration window, IIR, singular extension or counter-move history | Razoring, extended futility and internal iterative deepening around the per-direction line scores, and a cautious re-search of candidate wins (`search.cpp:445–460, 522–527`) | Null move, futility, razoring with VCF, aspiration windows, IIR, singular extensions, counter-move history, fractional depths adjusted for move type, complexity and policy score |
| TT | One entry per bucket, no leaf entries or cached evaluation | Three entries per cluster | Five entries per bucket; evaluation and quiescence results stored |
| Ordinary candidates | Chebyshev radius two, 24 surrounding cells per isolated interior stone | 16 line neighbours at radius two; wider candidates near the opening | Supplied config uses square radius three plus line radius four |
| Timing | Fixed budget; stop starting iterations after half the budget | Adapts to best-move changes, with a 70% iteration threshold | Optimum/maximum budgets and move-stability/value-change controls |

The local PentaZen source declares version 0.4.17 while its binary identifies itself as 0.4.18. The Rapfi binary's README calls it a single-threaded Gomocup 2025 build. Source comparisons describe available mechanisms; they do not assume these checkouts are exact source builds of the supplied binaries.

## Ranked findings

### 1. Evaluation has major development and initiative blind spots — highest priority

In `src/eval.rs:14–23`, the entire evaluation is a weighted sum of `five_cells`, `open_four_cells`, `four_cells` and `open_three_cells`, minus the opponent's sum. These count hypothetical next placements, not independent existing line patterns. Overlapping categories are added, weights are untuned, and all resulting scores are multiples of 100. Threes and twos used by move ordering are absent from static evaluation.

The audit probe confirms that a centre singleton, a corner singleton and a pair blocked at one end all score **0**, while an open pair scores **400**. The engine has some indirect appreciation of open twos because they enable future threes, but no comparable signal for many weaker or blocked structures. Central development, independent lines, useful connections and initiative have to emerge from search. There is no dynamic side-to-move threat term: evaluation simply changes sign when the side changes.

PentaZen values B3/F2/B2/F1/B1 material as well as B4/F3 (`external/pentazen/src/src/type.h:100` and `board.cpp:36`), tracks line scores incrementally, and averages the current and preceding state (`board.h:291`). Rapfi combines basic pattern evaluation with an 11-bit tactical situation table (`external/rapfi/src/Rapfi/eval/eval.cpp:33–116`) and can use the neural value evaluator. A richer classical evaluator is already a concrete improvement target; reproducing Rapfi's neural stack is a larger project.

### 2. Move scoring loses the information that makes a fork dangerous — highest priority

`src/threats.rs:37–102` ORs results from all four directions into the same masks. `MoveMasks::threat_score` then awards each category once (`src/search.rs:247`). A move making two blocked fours has the same four-category contribution as one making a single blocked four. A double open three likewise earns the open-three contribution once. Categories can combine, so a four-three is distinguished from a plain four; multiplicity within a category is lost.

The reproducible probe constructs a single four and two perpendicular fours at target `(7,7)`. Both have `four=true`, `open4=false`, `open3=false`. Their other ordering features can differ, but the actual fork information is absent from these masks. Search can recognize the double four after placement through two five-completion cells; this is an ordering/evaluation and search-cost deficiency, not a claim that the engine cannot ever see forks.

PentaZen sums directional material changes (`board.h:281`). Rapfi's `Pattern4` expressly identifies these combinations (`core/types.h:107–117`), and its `quickWinCheck` includes conditional four-three and double-three wins (`game/wincheck.h:54–108`). Fork classification should preserve counterthreat and shared-defence checks; arbitrary double threes are not unconditional wins.

### 3. The forcing horizon is shallow and expensive — highest priority

`Config::default` sets `leaf_depth=1`. Each attacking four decrements it (`src/search.rs:532`); forced blocks in the leaf are free. In the main search, however, a forced block of a four still consumes depth (`src/search.rs:562–577`). Only open-three responses are extended. This gives high5 a different and narrower tactical reach than the reference engines.

The leaf search tries all four-making cells in bit order, recomputes whole-board masks and evaluation, and has no transposition lookup/store. Approximately two thirds of counted nodes occur here. Simply increasing `leaf_depth` grows work without the localized candidates, combination filters and ordering that make the reference engines' longer VCF searches practical. The existing design notes already record larger limits collapsing completed depth.

At the same one-second protocol limit, freshly replayed positions show the practical consequence:

| Position, before high5's decision | Stones | High5 fresh search | PentaZen binary | Rapfi binary |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| `p00_high5_first`, decision index 8 | 20 | Depth 9, score −1200, `(9,8)` | Sole forced reply `(9,8)`; skipped substantive search | Reports loss in 22 plies, `(9,8)` |
| `p00_opponent_first`, decision index 3 | 9 | Depth 7, score −100, `(5,7)` | `(7,8)`, ordinary negative evaluation | `(7,8)`, ordinary negative evaluation |
| `p02_high5_first`, decision index 11 | 26 | Depth 5, score −1600, `(12,5)` | Reports loss in 22 plies | Reports loss in 18 plies, `(12,5)` |
| `p04_opponent_first`, decision index 4 | 11 | Depth 7, score −1600, `(9,5)` | Reports loss in 22 plies, `(10,4)` | Reports loss in 18 plies, `(10,4)` |

Coordinates and decision indices are zero-based. Positions were selected two high5 decisions before its first historical mate-loss report. Each query starts a fresh process/TT, unlike the historical game, so its scores/moves need not reproduce that game exactly. Reference mate reports are strong diagnostic evidence, not independently verified exhaustive proofs. These samples establish earlier tactical recognition; they do not identify the first irreversible mistake or prove that a different move still saves each position.

**Measured: forcing play moved into the main search; the double-threat extension is not a confirmed gain.** The same forcing play used to be handled three times — the static `decided` check, the free-reply rules in `node()`, and a depth-0 search over fours with its own budget that rebuilt the masks, ignored the transposition table and ignored the alpha-beta window. Now a forced reply keeps its depth, so the main search follows the chains itself, and a move that makes two threats at once is searched one ply deeper. The suite (which is all defensive positions) reads 20/21 with a reported depth of 4–7, unchanged by that extension and one position worse with the counter-threat answer set, so that answer set is off. The extension was then judged by matches and came out level: on openings 10–19 it took Tito from 4W–16L to 7W–13L, but on the fresh block 25–34 the same revision scored 3W–16L–1T against the pre-work control's 5W–15L, so over 40 games per arm the two revisions are 10W–29L–1T and 9W–31L. The extension is kept as principled and not measurably harmful, and the 3-win swing is recorded as a sample that did not replicate.

The forcing policy itself is inverted relative to both references. The reply to an open three costs no depth here, a relaxation that neither PentaZen nor Rapfi makes, while blocking a four costs a full ply, a move that both references extend: PentaZen adds one ply (`search.cpp:484–491`) and Rapfi adds 1.3 (`search.cpp:1299–1300`, `OPPO5_EXT` in `search/ab/parameter.h:61`). A continuous-four win of four attacking fours and four blocking replies therefore needs about eight plies of remaining depth to be seen, while the match evidence above has a mean nominal depth of 7.65 and only four of 232 decisions above depth 9. That is the same mechanism the design notes record from the other side, where the opponent's wins in the lost games were chains of 2–4 threats with forced replies, 7–11 plies long. The static rules are not the gap: `Threats::decided` (`src/search.rs:195–207`) covers the same decisive cases as PentaZen's `check_wld` (`board.cpp:469–496`) — a full board, our five next move, two opponent five-cells, and our own open four. Rapfi goes further and proves mates in three and five plies statically from its pattern counts — flex four, block-four-plus-flex-three and double flex three (`game/wincheck.h:34–111`) — and uses those to curtail search and to gate pruning; high5 has no static combination detector at all.

### 4. Aggressive pruning depends on those weak scores, and at equal time it is not yet earning its depth — high priority

At depth one the ordinary move limit is only **3**. Below the root's immediate children, `src/search.rs:617` can skip every move after the limit, including threatening moves, once a searched move is not mate-losing. Quiet moves also receive reductions; `quiet` ignores the three/two shape contribution (`src/search.rs:466`). A strategically useful developing move can therefore be classified as quiet and searched shallowly or discarded.

High5 borrowed PentaZen's move-count formulas, but not the whole surrounding scoring and verification machinery. The formulas themselves check out line by line: PentaZen's `FutilityMoveCount[0] = min(round(5*sqrt(d) - 2), 8)` for threatening nodes and `[1] = min(round(15*sqrt(d) - 12), 32)` for quiet ones (`search.cpp:84–86`), selected by `quietNode = is_quiet()` (a blocked four or an open-three threat for either side), which is the same split as high5's `tactical` flag. What is missing underneath is the per-direction SEE score that decides which moves fall inside the limit (`board.h:281–288`). The reductions are also not the aggressive part: high5 reduces only the moves it calls quiet (`src/search.rs:621`), while PentaZen reduces from the second move regardless of type (`search.cpp:499–519`), so what remains distinctive here is the skipping, not the reduction depth. PentaZen uses `cautious` re-search for candidate wins (`search.cpp:522–527`). High5 stops iterative deepening immediately on either mate sign and has no independent mate verification. There is a potential risk of selective mate misclassification; this audit did not demonstrate a false mate in the recorded games.

The fixed-depth audit isolates selectivity with TT, free replies and the leaf search disabled. On 60 deterministic sparse positions, eight end early with identical mate results and are excluded. Among the remaining **52**, scores differ in **21**, selected moves in **16**; selective search uses **31916** nodes against **279932** for full candidate search. One example changes the depth-three choice from `(3,5):5600` to `(6,7):4600`. This confirms substantive search changes and a substantial speed benefit. It does not by itself show whether disabling pruning improves equal-time results, so the second pass measured that on the same suite, at 1000 ms per position as one process per configuration:

| Configuration | Saving moves | Depths reached | Runs |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| default (`selective`, free three replies, `leaf=1`) | 18/21 | 5–9 | 3 |
| `no-selective` (move-count limit and LMR off) | **20/21** | 4–8 | 2 |
| `no-threes` (free replies to an open three off) | 19/21 | 7–12 | 1 |
| `leaf=3` | 19/21 | 5–8 | 1 |
| `no-tt` | 19/21 | 5–8 | 1 |

`selective` gates both the move-count limit and the reductions (`src/search.rs:603, 617, 621`); the `no-selective` row keeps the free three replies, the leaf search and the TT, so the difference is the pruning package alone. On this sample the package trades roughly one ply of nominal depth for two positions it stops saving. That is not a verdict on pruning in general — the sample is the tactical, loss-derived suite of finding 5, and PentaZen uses the same counts successfully because its ordering is more informative — but it does mean the limits are unproven here, and that they amplify the ordering errors of finding 2 instead of absorbing them. The implementation order therefore re-measures them before adding further search machinery.

**Measured again after the shape classes of finding 2 were added, with the two mechanisms separated.** `Config` now switches the limit and the reductions apart, so each was run on its own at 1000 ms per position: default (limit and reductions) 19/21 at depth 6–9, `no-reductions` 19/21 at 6–9, `no-limit` 19/21 at 5–8, `no-selective` 20/21 at 4–7. The score does not separate them, but depth and nodes do: on `Tito-p05_high5_first` the default completes depth 8 in 607 ms (475k nodes) where dropping the reductions needs the full 1000 ms and 765k nodes to reach depth 7, and on `Tito-p02_high5_first` the same drop costs a ply at equal time. So both mechanisms buy depth — the limit most of the two to three plies, the reductions about another ply on individual positions — and neither can be dropped without losing depth. The gate of implementation step 2 is still open on the suite (19/21 against 20/21), and a one-position gap is inside the resolution of 21 loss-derived positions whose labels come from a bounded prover: an exact four-to-seven-ply search is being compared with a selective six-to-nine-ply search, and this sample rewards exactness. A third variant was tried and rejected: making the limit skip quiet moves only, at every ply, so forcing moves are never dropped past it, costs a ply (6–9 → 5–8) and does not raise the score, which is why the type-blind skip at ply 2 and beyond stays. Closing the gate needs held-out matches (implementation step 9), not more tuning on this suite.

### 5. The passing tests and saving-position suite do not establish playing strength — high priority

All **51 tests pass**. Board/threat tests include independent grid oracles and scalar/AVX comparisons, so foundational corruption is not the leading explanation.

However, `agrees_with_minimax` calls `plain`, which disables selective pruning (`src/search/tests.rs:28–35,104`). The free-ply/leaf agreement test also sets `selective=false` (`:232`). The test enabling selectivity checks only that it visits fewer nodes (`:272`). The design document's claim that the *pruned* search agrees with minimax is inaccurate.

The current one-second position suite scores **18/21**, reproduced during this audit. It contains one duplicate, uses positions from earlier losses, and labels a move saving when an absent external prover failed to find a win within a bound. Thus it has 20 unique tactical positions, incomplete labels and no reproducible label-generation pipeline. Repeatedly tuning on it and on the same ten paired openings can favour patches that repair those positions while leaving development and defence weak. Existing logs already contain a 7–13 Tito run under another configuration; 20 games do not reliably rank close variants. The equal-time ablation table in finding 4 was run on this same suite and inherits the same bias: it shows the current pruning dropping saving moves on 21 loss-derived positions, not that pruning is wrong in general.

### 6. Repeated computation and limited TT coverage constrain useful depth — medium priority

Every main node builds both sides' five/open-four masks before the horizon test. Evaluation computes these again, plus both sides' four/open-three masks (`src/eval.rs:14`). Leaf expansion computes the attacking four mask again. Main move ordering eagerly builds all shape/threat masks, even for a node with a single forced block (`src/search.rs:590–600`). Horizon and leaf nodes bypass TT lookup entirely.

PentaZen and Rapfi incrementally update local line/pattern state. Rapfi also caches evaluation and VCF bounds. High5's AVX masks are independently tested and individually fast, but that does not establish that repeatedly building all of them is the best whole-search architecture. Reuse masks, bypass general ordering for single legal replies, instrument TT hits and profile wall time before choosing a full incremental rewrite.

The one-slot TT refuses to replace a deeper same-age entry even when its key belongs to another position (`src/search.rs:437`). Clustered replacement offers more flexibility. Free-ply caps also make cached values somewhat path-dependent; the existing design notes acknowledge this. Neither issue was isolated as a cause of a particular loss here.

Two hot-path costs are visible in the source. `MoveMasks::new` computes `three_and_two_cells` for both players at every interior node (`src/search.rs:227–244`), although the shape term it feeds is bounded by 480 points, and that mask is the most expensive of the set (four directions with five shifts each; `src/threats.rs:122–141`). Second, every mask test goes through `U256::words`, a 32-byte transmute (`src/avx.rs:20–28`) that `get`, `count_ones` and `pop_lowest` all call; ordering tests 11 masks per candidate (`threat_score` seven, `shape_score` four) over the 85–92 candidates a node has, about a thousand vector-to-scalar bit tests per interior node before any search happens. Measured throughput is about 0.80 million counted nodes/s in the match log. Note also that `Stats::leaf_nodes` counts the leaf routine's own recursion, so the leaf share is a node fraction and not a wall-clock share. The transposition table is 32 MB with one slot; PentaZen's is 256 MB with three entries per cluster (`tt.h:63`) and Rapfi's uses a five-tier bucket (`search/hashtable.h:35`), both with an evaluation or quiescence entry that high5 has no counterpart for.

### 7. Opening coverage, recent-move context and timing are secondary gaps

Ordinary candidate generation excludes every cell farther than two from all stones (`src/bitboard.rs:193`). PentaZen widens its opening range near the root with fewer than five stones (`movegen.cpp:177`); Rapfi's supplied configuration uses `square3_line4`. High5 simultaneously generates more off-line radius-two cells than PentaZen's default line neighbourhood. It can spend effort on weak nearby moves while excluding some wider development. A phase-dependent range deserves an ablation, rather than indiscriminately widening every node.

High5's recent-move proximity uses only the current hypothetical search path (`src/search.rs:446`). Real preceding game moves are never passed to the searcher. Near the root, PentaZen's last-two/last-four context therefore has more actual information than high5's imitation (`movegen.cpp:35–46`).

Timing stops new iterations after half the budget (`src/search.rs:390`), irrespective of move instability or score deterioration. **31/232** ordinary timed decisions return in under 600 ms. Average use is still 828 ms, so this is a secondary efficiency opportunity, not an explanation for the entire strength gap. PentaZen and Rapfi both manage stability and iteration timing explicitly.

### 8. The search heuristics Rapfi credits with large Elo gains are absent — later-stage gap

The mechanism table records this in one row; the second pass enumerated it because it bounds how much the search can gain once the position model is sound. High5 has PVS, killers and a history table indexed by cell, and nothing else: no null-move pruning, futility pruning, razoring, aspiration windows (every iteration searches `-INF..INF`, `src/search.rs:384`), internal iterative deepening, singular or ttMove/PV extensions, counter-move history, or reductions adjusted for move type. Rapfi annotates its own equivalents with the Elo its tuning attributed to each: futility pruning about 121, the move-count threshold about 107, the four-block response extension about 77, razoring with VCF about 68, policy-based reductions about 59, the singular extension about 52, the ttMove reduction about 32, and several smaller ones of 8 or less (`search/ab/search.cpp:882, 1129–1180, 1274–1365`). Those are that engine's own figures rather than measurements here, several depend on its learned policy scores, and none is worth attempting before findings 2 and 3 give the ordering something to work with, hence last in the order below.

## Recommended implementation order

1. Add per-direction pattern and combination information for move scoring and static evaluation. Include blocked/open weaker patterns, deduplicate overlapping descriptions, and preserve independent threats. Measure both development and tactical defence. Keep this first version classical and inexpensive. **Implemented.** `Shapes` in `src/threats.rs` classifies both players' stones per direction into five/open four/four/open three/blocked three/two, with the class precedence applied, and move ordering and the evaluation read the per-direction counts. The oracle suite now also checks the per-direction classes against a naive definition (600 random positions, both players, four directions, six classes), that the classes are disjoint within a direction, and that a crossing double four is counted in two directions. At 1000 ms per position the suite went 18/21 → 19/21 (depth 5–9 → 6–9) at the same ~0.8M nodes/s, and the evaluation probe reports 128 for a centre singleton (0 before), 121 for a blocked pair against 425 for an open pair, and 4784 as the peak absolute score over 200 random 30-stone positions against the 10000 clamp. The step 2 gate is still open: the default is 19/21 against 20/21 for `no-selective`.
2. Re-measure the pruning on the improved move model, and treat the limits as unproven until they pay: re-run the finding 4 ablation, require the default to beat `no-selective` at equal time on the suite and on held-out positions, and keep the limit and the reductions only where they do. While doing so, gate the move-count limit on move importance instead of skipping every move type past the limit at ply 2 and beyond (`src/search.rs:617`), skip the limit entirely when a node has one candidate, and re-check that `quiet` reflects the richer pattern information. **Measured; both kept.** The two mechanisms are now separate switches (`no-limit`, `no-reductions`) and were measured on their own: the limit carries most of the depth and the reductions about a ply on individual positions, so removing either loses depth without raising the suite score (finding 4 has the numbers). Gating the limit on move importance was implemented and rejected on measurement: it costs a ply and does not raise the score. The single-candidate case needs no exemption — the limit is never below 2, so the first move is always searched and the reductions start at the third. `quiet` was re-checked and deliberately left on the four forcing classes, so the richer classes changed the order of the moves without changing which ones the pruning may skip. Whether the default beats `no-selective` at equal time was unresolved then (19/21 against 20/21); after the continuous-four search of step 3 the same comparison is 20/21 against 17/21, so the pruning now pays on the suite.
3. Build a selective continuous-four search with ranked, localized continuation candidates and a clear distinction between found wins and inconclusive results. Add safe quiescence caching, then measure four-block extensions (Rapfi's 1.3-ply `OPPO5_EXT`) against the existing free-three policy, rather than stacking extensions without a depth/runtime comparison. **Implemented; what is left is narrow.** Forcing play lives in the main search (the separate depth-0 search is gone; the four block is free and a double-threat move is extended), which is the structure that lets threes be added in one place. What is in: **open threes are threats on the same footing as fours** — a three of ours narrows the opponent's answer to the block cells and our fours and makes it free, so the chain is followed and the win proven, and the opponent's three narrows ours the same way — and `the_default_settings_prove_wins_that_need_an_open_three` pins that down on the double-three position, which no fours-only search can win, with the fours case beside it. Caching is no longer separate work either: forcing nodes are ordinary nodes, so their results are stored and probed like any others. What is out: answering their open three with an open three of our own (measured worse on the suite, switch `no-counters`), Rapfi's exact reconstruction of a threat's real defences (we still use a superset, which adds candidates rather than missing them), and nothing else from this step. The double-threat extension is the only part that was ever credited with moving matches, and that credit is withdrawn: on openings 10–19 it took Tito from 4W–16L to 7W–13L, but on the fresh block 25–34 the same revision scored 3W–16L–1T against the pre-work control's 5W–15L, so over 40 games each the two revisions are 10W–29L–1T and 9W–31L — one game apart, no confirmed effect. It is kept as principled and not measurably harmful, not as a gain.
4. Add mate verification to the layer that finds wins: a cautious re-search before committing a win, no fail-low bound stored when moves were skipped, and continued deepening while a mate score is unverified. The recorded games show no false mate, but the pruning makes one cheap to produce silently and the suite cannot detect it. **Mate verification is in; the strict bound is in the code but off by default, because it costs.** `Searcher::verify_mate` re-searches a reported mate once with the move-count limit and the reductions off, at the **mate distance** rather than the iteration depth (verifying at depth 9 for a mate in 3 would never finish), and takes that answer when it disagrees; a test asserts every reported mate agrees with an independent exact search. **The follow-up audit (`docs/engine-audit-followup.md`, finding 4) measured what that check is worth, and it is less than the wording above implies:** with the table left on, the verification visits 120 nodes of which 119 come back as table cutoffs (reproduced here), and with the table off during verification the first replay spends about 766000 further nodes, hits the one-second deadline and still reports the original mate, because an interrupted verification returns `None` and the unverified result stands. Treat the verification as a second opinion rather than a proof, and read the follow-up audit for the tactical search and the open-three depth policy it shows to be missing — its worst case (a second spent in one extended subtree, iteration two entering a single root move) reproduces exactly here. The candidate bound rule — `store_tt` refusing a fail-low from a node that skipped moves — measured **4W–16L** against Tito where storing it measured **7W–13L**, and 19/21 against 20/21 on the suite, so it sits behind `strict-bounds` and is off. `Stats::skipped_bounds` counts how often it would fire, and a test checks that path with the switch on. Both figures are 20 games, which is about one standard error, so treat the size of the cost as indicative rather than measured.
5. Improve candidate coverage and context: a phase-dependent range (line neighbours at radius two, wider near the opening, as finding 7 describes) and recent-move proximity seeded with the real preceding moves of the game, which the protocol already has and the searcher never sees.
6. Reuse already computed masks, avoid general move scoring on single-block nodes, cache `evaluate` in the TT, and add leaf entries under a tag that keeps free-ply results out of main-search cutoffs. Profile the `U256::words`-based mask tests and `three_and_two_cells` before deciding whether incremental pattern maintenance is worth implementing.
7. Improve timing and stability: replace the fixed half-budget rule with best-move-stability and predicted iteration cost, and track `time_left` and `timeout_match`.
8. Then stack the heuristics of finding 8 one at a time, razoring with VCF and futility first, each measured against the previous step on held-out positions.
9. Evaluate each retained change on a larger, held-out set of paired openings at equal turn limits. Record engine revision/configuration, opening list, CPU/thread settings, terminal reason and full logs. Keep the current suite as a fast regression signal; judge strength by held-out matches. **How changes are measured now.** Matches cannot settle small changes — at a quarter win rate, 20 games per arm has a standard error of about 1.4 games, and a ten-point effect needs roughly 200 games per arm — so every change is measured in this order: (1) the tests, the checks in `examples/audit.rs`, the position suite for the default and for the switch the change touches, and depth and nodes at equal time read per position rather than as a range (a range hid a full ply during the pruning work); (2) a 20-game match on openings that were not used before, against `pbrain-pela.exe` and against Tito, before a change in search behaviour is kept, where two games either way is noise and four is worth investigating; (3) a big match (all 41 openings in both colours, 82 games per arm) only for a change expected to be large, with the threshold fixed before the run. Every run records its openings, engine hash and revision in `run-info.txt`. The first such match ran this way — openings 10–19 against Tito and 20–24 against pela, with the engine built from the revision before the shape work as the control — and gave after 5W–15L against before 4W–16L on Tito (8W–21L–1T against 7W–22L–1T over all 30 games per arm): nothing worse, and nothing this size of match can resolve. Both revisions lose the Tito games in about twenty moves per side, which points at finding 3 rather than at the ordering work of findings 1 and 2. **A second block was then run the same way for the work of step 3 and it is the reason that work is recorded as unproven:** openings 25–34 against Tito gave after (post step 3) **3W–16L–1T** against before (the pre-work control) **5W–15L**, reversing the 3-win swing the first block had shown on openings 10–19. Two blocks of 20 games per arm per revision are now the minimum before any match claim here, and the aggregate must be quoted rather than the better block. One of the forty Tito games hit the harness's 200-move cap and counts as neither a win nor a loss (opening 27, our engine first), and one of the pela games did too (opening 32, our engine first); the control had none in either arm. **Fresh-block totals: Tito after 3W–16L–1T against before 5W–15L, pela after 4W–15L–1T against before 5W–15L**, both arms on the same 20 games per opponent. **The two blocks must not be added together as one revision: the after side is two different builds** — `48922BBBF9C90D71` for openings 10–19/20–24 (7W–13L and 4W–6L on the first block) and `7E3119E2AB3C095A` for 25–34 (3W–16L–1T and 4W–15L–1T) — while the control is one build (`6B26D3EE04B1F1CA`) whose hash is recorded only for the fresh runs, so the earlier block's control build cannot now be proved. What the two blocks do support, separately and identically in direction: **no after build beat the control on its own block** (10–19: 7W against the stored 4W–16L control; 25–34: 3W–16L–1T against 5W–15L), so the search work of steps 1 to 4 moved no won game against these opponents on the evidence available, and the reason the next step is not another threat rule.

## Reproducing the audit

```powershell
cargo test
cargo run --release --example suite -- 1000
cargo run --release --example suite -- 1000 no-selective
cargo run --release --example audit
cargo build --release
python scripts/audit_engine.py --replay --ms 1000
```

`examples/audit.rs` reproduces the evaluation, fork-mask and fixed-depth pruning probes. The finding 4 table is reproduced with the suite's own switches (`no-selective`, `no-threes`, `leaf=3`, `no-tt`; see `Config::apply`), one process per configuration so that the timing and the transposition table start fresh. `scripts/audit_engine.py` validates the saved match, computes log statistics, checks paired openings and queries only its own local engine processes. It does not use Piskvork or alter registry settings. `docs/audit-evidence.json` retains structured match statistics, decisions, replay positions and raw engine responses. The saved diagnostic stdout is under the git-ignored `logs/audit-probes.txt` and `logs/audit-replay.txt`.
