# Design decisions

## Scope
- **Freestyle, 15×15 only.** `START` with any other size is answered `ERROR`. Piskvork defaults to 20×20, so its saved board size must be set to 15 (see README).
- **WebAssembly is a secondary target**, native x86-64 is primary.

## Board
- **Padded linear bitboard, one per player.** Cell (row, col) is bit `row*16 + col`; column 15 is always empty, so lines never wrap across rows and no edge masks are needed. The four directions are shifts of 1, 16, 15 and 17.
- **256-bit AVX2 words when available, `[u64; 4]` otherwise.** Selected at compile time (`Words` in `src/lib.rs`). On x86-64, `.cargo/config.toml` sets `target-cpu=native`; without AVX2 the scalar words are used, and they are the reference in tests and the fallback for WebAssembly.
- **Measured** (synthetic positions, one machine, one run): AVX2 was about 3× faster than `[u64; 4]` for win detection and about 4× for a whole-board five-completion mask; make/unmake was slightly slower (86 ns vs 53 ns for 60 operations).
- **Rejected:** `[u128; 2]` (slower than `[u64; 4]` on every operation) and four per-direction line boards (about 1.9× slower than AVX2 on completion masks).

## Threats
- **Masks are computed on demand from the bitboards**, not kept incrementally per cell as PentaZen and Rapfi do. Costs per player per position (30 stones, AVX2): five 54 ns, open four 59 ns, open three 160 ns, four 167 ns. Whether incremental updates would be cheaper is undecided until the search has a real node rate.
- **Open-four defence cells are a superset** of the real defences, which is safe (extra candidates, never missing ones).
- **Move ordering and the evaluation read per-direction shape classes** (`Shapes`), not the union masks the forcing rules use. A cell belongs to the strongest class it makes in one direction and the class is counted in every direction that shows it, so a double four counts twice: the check reports two directions for the crossing point against one for a single arm, where the old union mask said `four=true` for both. The classes also reach down to blocked threes and twos, which gives the evaluation a development signal it did not have (a centre singleton scored 0, now 128; a blocked pair 121 against 425 for an open pair), so blocked threes and twos now order moves while the *quiet* test still looks only at the four forcing classes, which keeps the pruning surface unchanged. At 1000 ms per position the suite went from 18/21 to 19/21 at the same node rate (~0.8M nodes/s), with about 20% fewer nodes at depth 8. The evaluation difference is halved so busy positions stay clear of the +-10000 clamp (peak 4784 over 200 random 30-stone positions); the alternative of halving each class weight was rejected because the extra truncation lost a position on the suite.
- **A double three or four-three is not a rule-based win.** It depends on the opponent's replies, so the search decides.

## Search
- **The Zobrist hash lives in `Board`**, updated by `make` and `unmake` with one XOR, so the search does not need `Game`. Keys are generated at compile time from a fixed seed (splitmix64), so hashes are reproducible between runs. The hash covers stones only; the search adds the side to move.
- **An interrupted iteration is never used.** The move always comes from a completed iteration (PentaZen uses partial results, Rapfi does not).
- **PVS was added after plain alpha-beta, mainly to separate full-window from null-window nodes.** On the suite (1 s per position) it changed mean depth from 5.67 to 5.76 and total nodes by under 1%. 90% of cutoffs come from the first move tried, so later moves are rarely reached. The null-window nodes then use the tighter move-count limit and one more reduction: mean depth 5.76 to 5.86, nodes down 4%. Both were small because about 73% of all nodes were in the leaf search (limit 3 at the time), which neither touches. The tighter limit was later tied to a four or open three on the board instead of to the null window. The limit formulas are PentaZen's and have now been checked against its source: `FutilityMoveCount[0] = min(round(5*sqrt(d) - 2), 8)` for a node with a four or an open three on the board and `[1] = min(round(15*sqrt(d) - 12), 32)` for a quiet one (`search.cpp:84-86`), selected there by `quietNode = is_quiet()`, which is the same split as the `tactical` flag here. Not borrowed is the per-direction SEE ordering that decides which moves fall inside the limit (`board.h:281-288`), and the reductions are milder here than there: only the moves this code calls quiet are reduced (against `search.cpp:499-519`). At equal time on the suite the pruning scores worse than switching it off, 19/21 against 20/21 at 1000 ms, while reaching about two plies more depth, so it is on trial. With the shape classes in place the two mechanisms are separate switches and each was measured alone: the limit carries most of the depth and the reductions about a ply on individual positions, so neither can simply be dropped; whether it pays can only be settled by matches, not by more tuning on this suite (finding 4 of the audit findings below).
- **Ordering scores every move from the shape classes** (both sides' fives, open fours, fours and open threes, counted per direction, plus blocked threes and twos that are ordered but do not count towards the quiet test); a move with none of the four forcing classes is "quiet" and is the only kind pruning and reductions touch. Keeping the quiet test on the four forcing classes alone means the richer model changed the order of the moves but not which ones the pruning may skip. On the suite, 81% of candidates at prunable nodes are quiet.
- **Transposition table: 32 MB, one slot per hash.** A deeper entry from the same search is not overwritten by a shallower one. A result from a deeper search can answer a shallower request, so with the table on the root score can differ from fixed-depth minimax; tests compare against minimax with the table off.
- **Search tests.** The search with the pruning and reductions switched off must equal plain minimax (same rules for decided positions, no ordering or table) at depth 2 and 3 on random positions; `plain` turns off the table, the free replies and the leaf search as well, the free-ply and leaf agreement test also forces `selective = false` (`:232`), and the test that turns selectivity on checks only that fewer nodes are visited (`:272`), so the pruned search is not itself compared against minimax. The decided-position rules are checked separately against minimax that ends only on a five or a full board. I checked that these tests fail for deliberate pruning bugs (searching only the first move; cutting off 150 points early); a cut-off 30 points early was not detected, because evaluations are multiples of 100.
- **Decided positions and the block-only reply to a four come from PentaZen and Rapfi** (`check_wld`, `quickWinCheck`). On the suite, nodes with a four or open three on the board that were not forced made 74% of all child nodes, searching 58-75 of about 85 candidates; these rules removed them. Depth in games did not change (about 7.3 at 1 s). Building the shared masks once per node instead of up to three times gained only about 6% in nodes per second, so the cause is probably that most removed children were instant losses that cost little (not measured).
- **Switches and counters live in two structs.** `Config` holds the switches and limits (one `Default`, one `apply` for command-line switches); `Stats` holds the counters and prints itself. A switch stays only while it is needed: for tests (an exact search to compare with minimax) or for a feature still being judged. Once a feature is measured and kept, its switch is removed, so the search does not branch on settled choices. PVS and move ordering have no switch for this reason.
- **Only the reply to an open three is a free ply.** Attacking moves cost depth. A free reply to a four was tried first; with the leaf search on it added nothing (suite: 17/21 with and without it, with free threes off) and cost about 0.8 plies of depth, so it was removed. PentaZen and Rapfi do free the reply to a four; neither frees the reply to an open three. Both also search four chains in a separate routine whose depth is not tied to the main search's remaining depth (PentaZen `min(2*ply, 120)`, Rapfi at every node whose depth has run out), and both give the four block extra depth (PentaZen one ply, Rapfi 1.3). A chain of four attacking fours and four blocks therefore needs about eight plies of main-search depth here, against the mean depth of 7.65 reached at 1 s, so it stays out of reach of the main search; the forced-reply rules instead let the main search keep its depth through the block, so a four and its block cost one ply together (finding 3 of the audit findings below).
- **The free reply to an open three is chosen from the loss analysis**, not from theory: the opponent's wins in the lost games were chains of 2-4 threats with forced replies, 7-11 plies long. On the position suite (1 s per position) switching it off cost 3 of 21 positions (17/21 against 20/21) and about 2 plies of depth (8.0 against 5.7 mean depth reached). The suite and the prover that built it use the same kind of reasoning, so this overstates real strength; matches are the test.
- **A forced node past the free-ply limit evaluates at depth 0.** Letting it keep expanding made chains unbounded (a bug found while testing).
- **A mate is verified before it decides a move; the stricter bound rule is off because it costs.** Step 4 of the audit had three parts. The one that survived measurement is the verification: `Searcher::verify_mate` re-searches a reported mate once with the move-count limit and the reductions off, at the **mate distance** rather than the iteration depth, because a mate reported at depth 9 may be a mate in 3 and an exact search of 9 plies would never finish; a test asserts every reported mate agrees with an independent exact search. Two limits belong next to that claim, and the follow-up audit measured both (the audit findings below): the re-search turns off the move-count limit and the reductions but **keeps the transposition table**, so it can settle on a bound the selective search itself stored, and it is **abandoned at the deadline** — `verify_mate` returns `None` and the caller keeps the unverified report. The verification is therefore a second opinion rather than an independent proof. I first wrote that verification at the iteration depth, which measured the same as not having it — that was a mistake in the implementation, not evidence about the idea. The second part, refusing to store a fail-low from a node that skipped moves, is a real correctness rule but it costs: Tito **4W–16L** with it against **7W–13L** without, and the suite 19/21 against 20/21. It sits behind `strict-bounds`, off by default, with `Stats::skipped_bounds` counting how often it would fire. Twenty games is about one standard error, so take the size as indicative — and note that the one figure in this work that was given a second block (the extension's 3-win swing) did not replicate, so a single block of this size is now treated as a hypothesis rather than a result.
- **Two threat rules were added and measured; neither one is a confirmed gain.** A move that makes two threats at once (a double four, or a four and an open three) is now searched one ply deeper (`double_threat_ext`, switch `no-double`), and the answer to an open three may include a counter-threat of our own (`counter_threes`, switch `no-counters`). On the suite the counter-threat answer scores **worse** (19/21 against 20/21) so it is off by default, and the extension is invisible (20/21 either way) because every one of those 21 positions is defensive — they were taken from games we lost two moves before the end. The matches are the instrument that can see an attacking change, and they did not confirm one. **The fresh block of openings (25–34) reversed the earlier hint.** On openings 10–19 the extension took the score against Tito from 4W–16L to 7W–13L, with pela unchanged at 4W–6L, and that looked like the first movement beyond noise this work had produced. On the fresh block the same revision scored **3W–16L–1T** against the pre-work control's **5W–15L** on the same 20 games. The extension is kept because it is principled (a double threat is the one case where the opponent's single answer cannot save them, so a ply spent there buys a whole reply) and because nothing measured worse, but the claim that it moved matches is withdrawn: it was 3 wins on 20 games and the next 20 did not repeat it. Over the two blocks the Tito arm therefore reads **10W–29L–1T for the after side against the control's 9W–31L** — one game apart, and not a single-revision figure, because the after side spans two builds (`48922BBBF9C90D71` then `7E3119E2AB3C095A`); the per-block comparison is the one to quote. This is exactly what the unused openings were for, and it is the reason a two-block sample is now the standard before any match claim here. The pela arm of the fresh block agrees: after 4W–15L–1T against before 5W–15L. **Neither block should be added to the other: the after side is two different builds** (`48922BBBF9C90D71` on openings 10–19/20–24, `7E3119E2AB3C095A` on 25–34), and the control's hash is only recorded for the fresh runs. What both blocks say, separately, is the same in direction: no after build beat the control on its own block, so the search work of steps 1 to 4 moved no won game against these opponents on the evidence available — and two of the fresh-block games for the newer build ran to the harness's 200-move cap while the control's none did. **The open-three half turned out to be in already**: a three of ours narrows the opponent's answer to the block cells and our fours and makes that answer free, exactly as a four does, so a three-based chain is followed and its win proven; `the_default_settings_prove_wins_that_need_an_open_three` pins this down with the double-three position, which no fours-only search can win. What is deliberately out is the counter-three answer (measured worse) and Rapfi's exact reconstruction of a threat's real defences; we use a superset of them, which adds candidates instead of missing them.
- **A move that makes two threats at once is extended, and it is capped by the same rule as everything else.** The extension is one ply, only for moves that make a second threat in a different direction (counted from the per-direction classes, no extra masks), and it is bounded by the depth counter and the deadline like the rest. Making forcing moves *free* instead of charging them was already rejected: a chain with zero cost per threat does not terminate. Until this step the same forcing play was handled three times: the static `decided` check, the forced-reply and free-ply rules inside `node()`, and a depth-0 search over fours that had its own budget, rebuilt the masks, and bypassed the transposition table and the alpha-beta window. Now a forced reply (a four block, or the answer to an open three) simply keeps its depth, so the main search follows the chains itself, bounded by `free_ply_limit`, and everything else about those nodes is ordinary. `decided` stays: it is constant time and stops work rather than repeating it. **The measurement that shows the duplication mattered**: while the depth-0 search existed, freeing the four block scored 19/21 against 20/21 without it (a loss); with the search gone it scores **20/21** against 19/21 with `no-fours` (a gain). The reported depth falls from 6–9 to 4–7 because each iteration now covers more plies, which is the expected artefact of extensions, not a loss of reach. On held-out openings the change is 4W–16L against Tito and 4W–6L against pela, against 5W–15L and 3W–6L–1T for the revision before: 8 wins in 30 games either way, so the match cannot separate them. Adding open threes to the forcing move set is the next step, and this structure is what makes it a change in one place instead of two.
- **Free-ply cap is measured from the root** and the time check is the real bound. Results found under a cap are then slightly path-dependent in the transposition table; this is accepted and to be measured.

## Protocol
- **Executable name `pbrain-high5`**: Piskvork requires the `pbrain-` prefix.
- **The engine is player 0, the opponent player 1**, whatever colour each plays. `BOARD` labels (1 own, 2 opponent) map to this directly.
- **Opponent moves that are out of range or on an occupied cell are answered `ERROR`** and the engine keeps running.
- **`INFO` is accepted and ignored for now.**

## Testing and evidence
- Bit-parallel code is checked against a separate naive scan over a plain grid on thousands of random positions, for both word types.
- Tests I wrote only show that code matches its stated definitions. Strength is judged by matches against Tito, PentaZen and Rapfi at equal time per move.
- The sources and binaries of those engines are kept in `external/` (git-ignored). Tito is closed source.
- **Measurement rules, because matches cannot settle small changes** (at a quarter win rate, 20 games per arm has a standard error of about 1.4 games). Every change is checked with the tests, the position suite (default and the switch the change touches) and depth and nodes at equal time read per position — a range across positions hid a full ply during the pruning work. Before a change in search behaviour is kept, a 20-game match on openings that were not used before, against `external\pela\pbrain-pela.exe` - the build the rating refers to, not Piskvork's bundled copy of the same version string - and against Tito, with two games treated as noise and four as worth investigating. A big match (all 41 openings, both colours, 82 games per arm) is for changes expected to be large, with the threshold fixed beforehand. Openings already used by the suite's source games or an earlier match are not reused for tuning; A/B runs compare binaries built from two revisions (a `git worktree` at the old revision is the control), never two engine settings, because Piskvork passes no switches to the engine; every run writes its openings, engine hash and revision to `run-info.txt`.
- **A change to the evaluation's scale is calibrated before it is played.** The futility margin, the null-move condition and the horizon return are all in the same units as the eval, so changing what the eval counts - `shape_score` now counts threat groups instead of the cells where a shape can be played, which had charged one threat two to four times - silently re-tunes the search. `scripts/retune.ps1` builds the shipped revision next to the working tree (stashing `src/eval.rs` and `src/search.rs`, then popping), runs the position suite and the game corpus with both, and prints nodes and `pruned` per position at equal depth. The class values were scaled 1.2x to put the eval's average magnitude back where the margin's game result was measured; at that scale the node count at equal depth is 0.974 of the shipped build's, and the build scored 53W-46L-1E against KANEC over 100 games at 1 s per move (shipped: 48W-47L-5E). Level to slightly ahead, not established.
- **Pruning is compared on quiet positions, not on the suite.** `eval_from_games` prints the sample's nodes, `pruned` and `pruned` per node at its fixed depth, with the clock out of it, and that is the only valid way to see what a change in the eval's scale or in the class ratios does to pruning. The position suite cannot do it: it is time-limited and tactical, so a ratio change alters which forced lines the search pursues and the node count moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the margin - it read 1.122 of the shipped revision at one scale and 1.335 at a smaller one. Reference, current build (counting fix, 1:1 four-to-open-three, `[3240, 1620, 162, 162, 27, 9]`) on 41 sampled quiet positions at depth 5: **11,023,493 nodes, 268,866 per position, pruned/node 2.159**.
- **The first such match changed how the next steps were ordered.** Openings 10–19 against Tito and 20–24 against pela, control built from the revision before the shape work: after 8W–21L–1T, before 7W–22L–1T over 30 games per arm. Nothing worse, and nothing a 30-game match can resolve; both revisions lose the Tito games in about twenty moves per side, so the ordering and evaluation work of findings 1 and 2 did not move the outcome and the forcing horizon of finding 3 is where the losses are.

## Audit findings, folded from the deleted audit documents (16 October 2026)

the audit findings below, the audit findings below, the audit findings below and their scripts, examples and evidence files were deleted on 16 October 2026. The measurements that still decide something are collected here; the rest was superseded or reproducible from what remains. Where a bullet above says "the audit" or "finding 3/4", it means this section.

- **A verified mate is only a second opinion.** `verify_mate` re-searches a reported mate at the mate distance with the move-count limit and the reductions off but with the table on: the check visits about 120 nodes, of which about 119 come back as table cutoffs. With the table off during the check the first replay spends roughly 766,000 further nodes, hits the one-second deadline and still reports the original mate, because an interrupted check returns `None` and the unverified result stands. `mate_verified` in the result and the `DEBUG` line say which of the two happened.
- **Refusing to store a fail-low from a node that skipped moves is correct but costs.** With it, Tito 4W-16L where storing it measured 7W-13L, and the suite 19/21 against 20/21. It sits behind `strict-bounds`, off; `Stats::skipped_bounds` counts how often it would fire. Both figures are 20 games, about one standard error.
- **The move-count limit and the reductions are separate switches because they do different amounts of work.** The limit moves most of the depth, the reductions about a ply on individual positions, so neither can simply be dropped; whether they pay at all is settled by matches, not by the suite, which reads 17/21 for `no-selective` against the default's 21/21.
- **Charging the reply to an open three its ply fixes a real defect and changes no game outcome.** `no-three-ext`: suite 16/21 against 21/21, but over 40 games on openings 25-34 against Tito and pela it scored 8 wins against the default's 7 and the pre-work control's 10. It stays off.
- **The double-threat extension's match evidence did not replicate.** Openings 10-19 took Tito from 4W-16L to 7W-13L; the fresh 25-34 block then scored 3W-16L-1T against the control's 5W-15L, i.e. 10W-29L-1T against 9W-31L over 40 games per arm. Kept as principled, not as a gain.
- **The free-ply cap is measurable and the default is the right end of it.** Suite 21/21 at the default cap 64 and at cap 24 and 12, 18/21 at cap 8; on games, cap 12 scored 2W-18L and 0W-20L against Tito where the default scored 3W-17L and the 80-game arm 23W-80 on the same openings.
- **Local optimisation.** Five changes were prototyped, three are in the engine, two are not. Applied: the deferred static evaluation (computed after the table probe and only when `!root && selective && !forced && (futility || razor)`, which is the guard set of all its readers), the read-only set-bit iterator (`Bits::words`/`Bits::bits`, used by `Scores::new` and the two ordering builders) and the bulk discard of the pruned tail at `ply >= 2`. All three preserve every move, score, depth and `Stats` field. The audit measured the five together at **16.1% less search time at the median of six paired runs**, on its own corpus at cap 12 with mate verification off, so that is not a production figure; on the changed tree at depth 5 against a 482.14 ms baseline the bulk discard alone measured **446.29 ms (-7.4%)**, the largest remaining delta and why it was the third change applied. Not applied: the per-node double-threat mask (a sign flip, -4.5% in the audit against +5.0% re-measured), compact `Shapes` (memory only, 3,072 -> 1,920 bytes with the layout check confirming it, but 2.7% slower) and narrowing `Scores::shape` to `i16` (bound 1,800, never benchmarked). Type sizes measured then: `Shapes` 3,072, `Scores` 2,048, `MoveList` 1,816, `Move` 8, `Entry` 16, `Board` 128, `Stats` 1,808, `Searcher` 4,720 bytes.
